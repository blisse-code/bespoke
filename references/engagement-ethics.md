# Engagement ethics: the gate on deliberate-inaccuracy tactics

Gravitas already gates this technique for one-to-one contexts (interviews, networking, negotiation). The content-formula source material for this skill recommends the same underlying technique for a different context entirely: published social posts read by a large, anonymous audience. That's a bigger version of the same problem, worth its own explicit treatment here rather than a one-line pointer.

---

## The technique, named plainly

Stated two ways across the source material: "Trigger Correction" (state something you know is slightly inaccurate or controversial, so the comment section fills with corrections) and "a firm, slightly provocative line to invite replies or corrections." The mechanism is identical to Deliberate Disbelief, already evaluated in Gravitas: say something wrong on purpose, let the audience's compulsion to correct you generate engagement.

## Why the published-content version is a bigger deal, not a smaller one

In a one-to-one conversation, one person gets misled and can ask a follow-up in real time. In a published post, an unknown number of readers see a claim stated as if the author believes it, some of them will not see or read the correction thread, and some fraction will walk away having absorbed the false claim as the author's actual position. The "it generates engagement" upside is real. So is the cost: readers who trusted the post at face value were deceived on purpose, and the ones who never see the corrections in the replies never get un-deceived at all.

## The rule

1. A genuinely provocative but honestly-held position ("most roadmaps are theater and here's why") is not gated. That's just a strong opinion, stated plainly, which is what Conviction (Section 2 of SKILL.md) already asks for.
2. A claim the writer knows to be inaccurate, stated specifically to bait corrections, is gated. Don't include it by default. If the person specifically asks for this tactic and understands that it means readers get corrected rather than informed, that's their call to make deliberately, not something this skill reaches for automatically because it's proven to drive comments.
3. When in doubt about which of the two a draft's provocative line actually is, ask: does the writer actually believe this, or are they saying it because it's wrong in a useful way? If it's the second one, flag it rather than including it silently.

This doesn't ban strong opinions, sharp takes, or genuine controversy. It bans manufacturing a specific factual error as bait. Those are different things and it matters not to conflate them.
