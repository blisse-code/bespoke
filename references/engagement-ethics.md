# Engagement ethics: the gate on deliberate-inaccuracy tactics

Rapport-building that works by naming what a reader is likely feeling is honest and always available. This file governs a different, narrower technique: stating something deliberately inaccurate specifically to provoke a correction. That works by the reader not knowing what's happening, which puts it in a different category from everything else in this skill and it earns its own explicit treatment.

---

## The technique, named plainly

Sometimes called "Trigger Correction" or elicitation via deliberate disbelief: state something you know is wrong or overstated, and let the compulsion to correct it generate replies. It works. It works because it deceives.

## Why published content raises the stakes, not lowers them

In a one-to-one conversation, one person gets misled and can ask a follow-up in real time. In a published post, an unknown number of readers see a claim stated as if the author believes it, some of them never see the correction thread, and some fraction walk away having absorbed the false claim as the author's actual position. The "it generates engagement" upside is real. So is the cost: readers who trusted the post at face value were deceived on purpose, and the ones who never see the corrections never get un-deceived at all.

## The rule

1. A genuinely provocative but honestly-held position ("most roadmaps are theater and here's why") is not gated. That's a strong opinion, stated plainly, which Section 2's plain-style discipline already asks for.
2. A claim the writer knows to be inaccurate, stated specifically to bait corrections, is gated. Don't include it by default. If the person specifically asks for this and understands the trade-off, that's their deliberate call, not something this skill reaches for automatically because it drives replies.
3. When unsure which of the two a provocative line actually is, ask: does the writer believe this, or are they saying it because it's wrong in a useful way? If the second, flag it rather than including it silently.

This doesn't ban strong opinions, sharp takes, or genuine controversy. It bans manufacturing a specific factual error as bait. Those are different things, and it matters not to conflate them.
