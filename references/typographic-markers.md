# Typographic and formatting markers

Section 1 of `SKILL.md` treats punctuation and formatting habits as part of a person's voice fingerprint, the same category as function-word choices: mostly unconscious, mostly stable, and genuinely diagnostic of who wrote something. This file is the detail behind that claim, applied to the specific typographic habits that default-generated text gets wrong most often.

The frame matters: none of the items below are banned outright. Each is a *default calibration*, what to assume absent a sample that says otherwise, because each is a pattern default-generated text reaches for at a much higher and more uniform rate than real individual writers do. A real sample that shows otherwise overrides the default every time; that's the whole point of Section 1 existing.

---

## Em dashes and en dashes

**The default:** absent a sample, treat em dashes as rare. This is the single most recognizable typographic tell in circulation, not because em dashes are wrong (working writers and editors use them constantly) but because default-generated text uses them at a rate and in a rhythm (setting off an aside mid-sentence, especially in pairs) that's far more uniform than any individual writer's actual habit.
**The calibration:** if the voice sample uses em dashes regularly, that's the writer's real habit, keep it. If no sample exists, default to periods, commas, or parentheses instead, and let the writer's first correction (if they add em dashes back) become the first real data point for their profile.

## Curly quotes vs. straight quotes

Minor on its own, many ordinary editors auto-curl quotes by default, so this is not diagnostic alone. Only worth normalizing to straight quotes when it's one signal among several in a cluster (see the cluster principle at the end of this file).

## Title Case headings

**The default:** sentence case ("Why this matters now"), not Title Case ("Why This Matters Now"). Title-casing every heading word is a stylistic default that shows up constantly in generated text and inconsistently in individually-written work, most people don't apply it uniformly across every heading without a style guide telling them to.

## Boldface and emphasis density

**The default:** functional, not decorative. Bold marks something the reader needs to find again, not every noun phrase that feels important while writing. A paragraph where every third phrase is bold reads as a formatting habit applied mechanically, not a set of genuine emphasis choices.

## Inline-header bulleted lists

**The pattern:** a list where every item opens with a bolded restatement of its own topic before the actual content ("**Performance:** Performance was improved by..."). This is padding wearing a list's clothing: the bolded lead adds nothing the sentence after it doesn't already say. Either write the list as plain prose, or drop the bolded lead and let the content stand on its own.

## Hyphenated compound overuse

**The pattern:** hyphenating a compound even in predicate position, where natural usage typically drops it: "the report is high-quality" instead of "the report is high quality," "the team is cross-functional" instead of "the team is cross functional." Keep the hyphen when the compound modifies a following noun ("a high-quality report"); drop it when the compound follows the noun it describes. This is a genuinely subtle rule most style guides don't state explicitly, which is exactly why uniform hyphenation in every position is a tell: it reflects a rule being applied mechanically rather than a native ear making the distinction.

## Emoji

**The default:** none, unless the voice sample or the platform convention clearly calls for them. Emoji used to mark section headers or bullet points ("🚀 **Launch Phase**") is close to always a formatting habit, not a genuine expressive choice; emoji used sparingly and specifically, the way a person actually texts, is a different thing entirely and should follow the sample, not this default.

---

## The cluster principle, restated for formatting

One item from this list, alone, tells you almost nothing: writers who use em dashes exist, so do writers who Title-Case their headings out of habit from another context. What's diagnostic is a cluster: several of these defaults violated at once, alongside a cluster of the word-and-phrase-level patterns in `plain-style-diagnostics.md`. A draft with heavy em-dash use, Title Case headings, three bolded phrases per paragraph, and a "delve into" is a confession. Any one of those alone is just a writer's ordinary habit.
