# Tell taxonomy (general): what reads as generated, for any voice

A leaner, voice-agnostic version of the discipline Marrow applies specifically to Chiranjeet's brand voice. Organized the same way, for the same reason: a single hit rarely matters, a cluster is the actual signal.

---

## Content-level tells

**Significance inflation.** "Marks a pivotal moment," "stands as a testament to." Claiming an ordinary fact represents something larger than itself. Replace with the plain fact and let it stand on its own.

**Promotional inflation.** "Vibrant," "stunning," "breathtaking," "nestled in," "a must-visit." Advertising language dressed as description. If it wouldn't survive being said out loud to a friend, cut it.

**Vague attribution.** "Experts believe," "studies show," without naming who or which. Either name the source or cut the claim. This matters even more here than in most writing advice: current AI classifiers are specifically trained on this exact pattern, because it's one of the most common ways generated text manufactures borrowed authority.

**Formulaic "despite challenges" close.** A challenges paragraph that resolves into vague optimism instead of a specific outcome. Replace with the actual trade-off that was made.

## Grammar-level tells

**Copula avoidance.** "Serves as," "boasts," "features," used where "is" or "has" would be plainer. Prefer the plain verb.

**Synonym cycling.** Renaming the same referent every sentence to avoid repetition ("the founder... the entrepreneur... the visionary..."). Repeating the plainest word is more human than cycling synonyms.

**False ranges.** "From X to Y" used to sound sweeping when X and Y aren't actually on a meaningful scale. List the actual things instead.

**Rule-of-three padding.** Before keeping a triad, ask: does each item carry distinct, checkable weight, or would the sentence lose nothing with two items or four? "Innovation, inspiration, and insight" fails this test.

## Structural tells

**Manufactured metaphor stacking.** One vivid comparison, earned, lands. Three across one short piece reads as trying too hard, and it's the same instinct that makes AI-generated marketing copy sound hollow.

**Manufactured punchline runs.** A single short declarative sentence for emphasis is fine. Three or more in a row, each performing the same "big reveal," is a recognizable pattern now, precisely because it's the easiest way to fake human rhythm without earning it.

**Aphorism formulas.** "X is not a Y, it's a Z." "X is the language of Y." Portable across any topic with the nouns swapped, which is exactly why it reads as generated rather than argued. Replace with the actual mechanism behind the claim.

**Signposting.** "Let's dive in," "here's what you need to know." Announces the move instead of making it.

**Fragmented headers.** A heading immediately followed by a one-line paragraph that just restates the heading before real content starts. Cut the restatement.

## Filler and hedging

**Filler phrases.** "In order to" (to), "due to the fact that" (because), "at this point in time" (now).

**Excessive hedging.** "Could potentially possibly" stacked qualifiers on one claim. Say the actual confidence level once.

**Generic non-conclusions.** "The future looks bright," "exciting times ahead." Replace with the actual next specific thing.

---

## What NOT to flag

- A genuinely short sentence that lands a real point. The tell is the run of three or more, not the existence of one.
- Domain-necessary vocabulary that happens to sound like a buzzword ("cross-functional," "data-driven") when it's the accurate term for the thing being described, not decoration.
- An owned phrase or personal metaphor the writer has genuinely used before and would recognize as theirs. Check the voice profile before assuming a distinctive phrase is a tell.
- Confident, qualifier-free language in general. That's the goal, not the problem. The tell is decorative filler, not directness.
- A rougher, slightly imperfect line sitting inside an otherwise polished draft. Uniform perfection across an entire piece is itself a tell (an absence of natural variation); one rougher line is often the most human line in it.

## The cluster principle

One hit proves nothing. A paragraph with one hedge, one vivid image, and one rule-of-three that actually carries distinct weight is just competent writing. The signal is a cluster: several of the above stacking in the same passage, reinforcing each other, until the piece reads as assembled rather than written.
