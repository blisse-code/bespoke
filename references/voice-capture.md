# Voice capture: building a profile that outlasts one message

Humanizer's voice calibration reads a sample once, per request, and matches surface rhythm for that one rewrite. That's useful but thin. This file builds something that persists across a conversation and gets more accurate the more the person writes.

---

## What to extract from a sample

Read 2-3 paragraphs of the person's own writing (or more if given) for:

- **Sentence-length pattern.** Short and punchy? Long and winding? Genuinely mixed? Measure it, don't guess.
- **Vocabulary register.** Casual, technical, academic, some specific blend? Note actual words they reach for, not a generic label like "casual."
- **Paragraph openings.** Do they jump straight into the point, or set a scene first? Do they ever open with a fragment?
- **Punctuation habits.** Heavy on em dashes, parentheticals, semicolons, ellipses? (If the sample is heavy on em dashes, that's their real habit; it does not override a platform-specific hard ban elsewhere, like Marrow's 1.1, if that skill also applies. Flag the tension rather than silently picking one.)
- **Recurring phrases or verbal tics.** Words or constructions they return to. These become candidate owned phrases if they show up more than once unprompted.
- **How they handle transitions.** Explicit connectors, or do they just start the next point?
- **What they get specific about.** Not just how specific they are, but which topics pull real detail out of them. That's where their best material lives.

## Building the commonplace book

Over a conversation, keep a running, informal list of the person's own resonant phrases, real details they've mentioned (a client name they've cleared to use, a real number, a real place), and recurring metaphors they reach for unprompted. This is their raw material, not a generic swipe file. Draw from it before inventing a comparison or example from scratch. A metaphor the person has used before and would recognize beats a more polished one Bespoke generated fresh.

## When no sample exists yet

Ask for one before asking for adjectives. "Confident and direct" describes a thousand different voices. Two real paragraphs disambiguate instantly. If the person genuinely has nothing to paste (a first-time user, a brand-new platform), draft from their stated adjectives, keep the draft deliberately plain rather than reaching for a generic "confident LinkedIn voice," and treat the first draft as the seed of their profile: ask afterward what to keep or change, and bank the answer for next time.

## Updating the profile

Voices drift. If a later sample or a later correction contradicts an earlier read of the voice, the more recent material wins. Note the drift rather than silently overwriting; if the person's tone has shifted noticeably (more formal, more relaxed, more technical), that's worth surfacing to them directly rather than quietly picking one version.
